Computer experiments & uncertainty quantification

Learning more
from fewer
simulations.

I develop statistical methods that turn limited simulation runs into useful predictions, uncertainty estimates, and better decisions.

My research connects Gaussian process emulation, multi-fidelity simulation, dynamic modeling, and active learning to make complex computer models more useful for scientific discovery.

Why this research matters ↓
01 The motivation

What if the experiment
is too costly to run?

Real world experiments are often too dangerous, expensive, or time consuming to perform. Testing an aircraft under extreme conditions, investigating how an infectious disease might spread, or studying the effects of a changing climate may require experiments that are difficult or impossible to conduct directly.

Computer simulations offer another way to investigate these questions. They use mathematical models and numerical algorithms to describe a system and explore how it responds to different conditions. A computer experiment studies these simulations systematically by choosing inputs, running the model, and analyzing its outputs.

01 / Engineering

How will a design perform?

How does a system function?

What could happen under change?

Compare scenarios for climate, water resources, and other evolving environmental systems.

The statistical challenge

Simulations can be
expensive, too.

A detailed simulation may take hours or days, and scientific questions rarely require just one run. Exploring unknown parameters, comparing designs, or assessing uncertainty can require many evaluations. A limited computing budget therefore raises two questions. How can we predict a simulator's behavior without running it everywhere? And which runs are worth performing?

I address these questions through statistical emulators, especially Gaussian processes. An emulator learns the relationship between inputs and outputs from a selected set of runs, provides fast predictions at new inputs, and quantifies uncertainty about those predictions.

01 / Multi-fidelity simulation

How can models of different
accuracies work together?

A coarse computational mesh or a simplified physical model can produce inexpensive approximations, while a more detailed simulation may be much more costly. Multi-fidelity methods combine these sources so that inexpensive runs help us learn where detailed simulations are most valuable.

I develop Gaussian process emulators that capture nonlinear relationships across fidelity levels. My work includes the Recursive Non-Additive model and the Diffusion Non-Additive model for simulations with tunable precision. The latter also estimates the limiting response as a numerical precision parameter approaches zero, a target that may be too expensive to compute directly.

02 / Dynamic simulators

How can we predict a system
as it evolves over time?

Many simulators describe trajectories rather than a single output. Examples include interacting populations, oscillating mechanical systems, and the evolution of physical states. Emulating these systems requires us to account for how uncertainty at one time affects predictions at later times.

I develop emulators that learn how a system moves from its current state to the next, then apply this relationship recursively. Using Gaussian approximations during propagation, this approach computes predictive means and variances analytically rather than estimating them by repeated sampling. The goal is efficient trajectory prediction with an explicit account of uncertainty.

03 / Uncertainty quantification

What do we know,
and how certain are we?

A fast prediction is most useful when we also understand its limitations. Uncertainty quantification asks how uncertain inputs, incomplete simulation data, and modeling assumptions affect the conclusions we draw.

Even a deterministic simulator can have an uncertain emulator prediction because we have observed only a finite number of runs. This uncertainty is different from the discrepancy between the simulator and the physical world. My research studies predictive uncertainty and its propagation, including how prior choices for Gaussian process parameters affect inference and prediction.

04 / Active learning & Bayesian optimization

Which simulation
should we run next?

When each run has a cost, the choice of where to evaluate a simulator matters. Active learning uses the current emulator to select new runs, updates the model with the results, and repeats. Active learning can target improved prediction or reduced uncertainty, while Bayesian optimization targets a design or input that performs well.

My work develops active learning strategies that select both the input and fidelity of the next simulation. An ongoing direction is efficient Bayesian optimization for networks of connected simulators, where the output of one component becomes the input to another. These settings require us to consider both the value of a new evaluation and its computational cost.
